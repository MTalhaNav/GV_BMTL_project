The story

A bit of detective work, in six steps.

  1. 1

    We start with a blank page

    Nothing yet, just an empty grid of pixels . We are turning a tilted page back into a flat one.

    Read more in detail

    The scan is a grid of output pixels: 1100 wide, with the height set by the page shape. The pixel buffers are made once in warp.js and reused, so dragging never asks for new memory.

    While you drag, a smaller preview is drawn. Its size adapts to your device, between 0.25 and 0.75 of full size, aiming at about 10 ms per frame. 150 ms after you stop, the full-size scan replaces it.

  2. 2

    Each pixel asks a question

    For every pixel on the flat page: where should I look in the photo to find my colour? But we have no directions yet. We need 8 numbers.

    Read more in detail

    The map sends a spot (u, v) on the flat page to (x, y) in the photo:
    x = (h0·u + h1·v + h2) / (h6·u + h7·v + 1)
    y = (h3·u + h4·v + h5) / (h6·u + h7·v + 1)

    The shared bottom line is the perspective. When h6 and h7 are zero it equals 1, and the map is a plain stretch, turn or slant.

  3. 3

    The clues come from you

    Drag 4 dots onto the page corners. Each corner gives 2 clues, one for left and right and one for up and down. 4 corners give 8 clues, exactly enough to find 8 unknown numbers.

    Read more in detail

    The four dots, top left, top right, bottom right and bottom left, give 8 equations for the 8 unknowns h0 to h7 of a homography (the matrix). The ninth number, h8, is fixed at 1, because the matrix is only defined up to scale.

    Before solving, the dots are checked: the cross products of consecutive edges must all be positive (a convex shape, in clockwise order), and the area must be big enough. This rejects bow-ties, dents and a wrong order. The engine alone would happily return numbers for a bow-tie.

  4. 4

    Solving the clues into a map

    We solve them with Gaussian elimination , the elimination method from school, just bigger. The 8 numbers it finds are our map: like a compass, they tell each pixel which way to look in the photo, and how far.

    Read more in detail

    numbers_looker builds the 8 × 8 system and solve_numbers solves it with Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting: in each column, the row with the biggest number is swapped to the top before the rows below are cleared, then back substitution finds h7 up to h0.

    If even the biggest number in a column is below 0.0000000001, the dots are unusable and it returns null instead of dividing by almost nothing. The solver runs again on every frame of a drag.

  5. 5

    Painting pixel by pixel

    The engine goes to each pixel of the flat page, uses the map to find its spot in the photo, notes the colour there and paints it in. Pixel by pixel, until the page is complete.

    Read more in detail

    Backward mapping, what real scanners do: the loop runs over the output pixels, and each one looks up its source in the photo. It samples at pixel centres (i + 0.5) and blends the four nearest photo pixels (bilinear sampling).

    Along each row the two top lines and the bottom line of the formula change by a fixed amount, so they are updated by addition, with just one division per pixel.

  6. 6

    Why go backwards?

    If we pushed photo pixels forward onto the page instead, some spots would never get a colour and the page would have holes. Asking backwards fills every pixel.

    Read more in detail

    Forward mapping, shown only to demonstrate the problem: the loop runs over the photo pixels inside the dots’ bounding box and pushes each one to where it lands, using the photo-to-flat matrix. Scan pixels that nothing lands on stay see-through, which shows the gaps.

    It needs the bottom line of the formula to be above zero, to ignore points on the far side of the vanishing line. It always renders at full size, because the gaps need it.

The extras

Three more things the scanner can do, and what each one shows.

  1. 1

    Greyscale: only the colour changes

    Every pixel stays exactly where it is. Its colour is pulled towards its own brightness, counting green most because our eyes are most sensitive to it, so the page turns into shades of grey.

    Read more in detail

    Greyscale uses whole-number brightness: (77·red + 150·green + 29·blue) / 256, then mixes each colour towards it by a fixed-point amount. Black and white is one comparison per pixel; 0 means off, and it overrides greyscale.

    Both work on the flattened scan only, after the warp, so they never move a pixel.

  2. 2

    Plain crop: why cutting is not enough

    Cutting out the upright box around the dots looks like a quick fix, but the page inside is still tilted and the desk shows in the corners. That is why a scanner has to stretch the page, not just crop it.

    Read more in detail

    Plain crop copies the smallest upright rectangle around the four dots, the same box Geometry.boundingBox finds. No matrix is involved, which is exactly why the page inside stays tilted.

  3. 3

    Forward mapping: going the wrong way

    Instead of each scan pixel asking where to look, every photo pixel is pushed to where it lands. Some spots are hit twice and some never, so the page is left full of holes.

    Read more in detail

    This switches on the forward loop described in step 6 of the story. Each photo pixel is pushed forward with the photo-to-flat matrix, and every scan pixel that is never hit stays see-through.

    Pixels that are hit twice just keep the last colour, so stretched areas get holes and squashed areas waste work.

How it works

There are Four parts to this Trick

1st

Four dots, eight equations

Each dot has an x and a y, and each of those is one equation. The matrix has eight unknown numbers, so four dots are exactly enough. Gaussian elimination solves them.

Your dots nowwaiting for the photo

2nd

One matrix sends any scan pixel to the photo

x = (h0·u + h1·v + h2) / (h6·u + h7·v + 1)
y = (h3·u + h4·v + h5) / (h6·u + h7·v + 1)

(u, v) is a spot on the flat page and (x, y) is where it sits in the photo. The shared bottom line is the perspective. Drag the dots into a parallelogram and it drops to zero.

Perspective termswaiting for the photo

3rd

Ask backwards, not forwards

For each scan pixel the matrix says where to look in the photo, and the colour is blended from the four nearest photo pixels. Pushing photo pixels forward instead leaves gaps.

4th

Then one rule per pixel

Greyscale pulls each colour towards its brightness. The black and white cut-off turns every pixel black or white around one number. Neither changes the shape.

Going deeper

Why we built it this way, in plain words.

Why eight numbers and not nine

The map is really a grid of nine numbers, but only their sizes compared to each other matter. If you doubled all nine, every pixel would still land in exactly the same place, because the doubling cancels out when the formula divides.

A recipe works the same way: 2 cups of flour to 1 of sugar makes the same cake mix as 4 to 2. So we fix the last number at 1 and only solve for the other eight. That is why 4 corners, with 2 clues each, are exactly enough.

Why the scan looks smooth, not blocky

When a pixel of the scan asks the photo for its colour, the answer is almost never exactly on one photo pixel. It usually falls somewhere between four of them.

Copying just the nearest one would make edges look jagged, like stairs. Instead we mix the four neighbours, giving more weight to whichever is closest. We also measure from the middle of each pixel, not its corner, so the whole picture doesn't shift by half a pixel.

While you drag, we draw a smaller version first so it stays fast, then swap in the full-size scan a moment after you stop.

What goes wrong when the dots are wrong

The formula divides by a number that depends on where you are on the page. If the dots are placed badly, that number can reach zero somewhere, and dividing by zero sends the pixel off to infinity.

As long as the four dots make a proper page shape, with no edges crossing and no corner pushed inwards, that number never reaches zero. So when the shape folds in or crosses itself, the outline turns red and the scan pauses until you fix it.

What we left out: finding the corners automatically

Scanner apps on your phone find the page corners for you. They look for strong edges in the photo and pick the biggest four-sided shape.

We left that out on purpose. Finding the corners is a different problem from flattening the page, and if the app guessed wrong it would hide the maths this page is meant to show. Here, you are the corner finder.

Under the hood

One place for settings. Every choice you make, like the dots, Enhance or black and white, is stored in one settings object in main.js. The drawing code reads from there, never from the buttons directly.

One redraw per frame. If several things change at once, invalidate() collects them and redraws once, on the screen's next refresh. That stops the page from doing the same work many times.

Four stages. The page goes through start, demo, ready and moved. If you grab a dot while the opening animation plays, the animation stops and hands control to you.

Downloads are always full quality. Download redraws the scan at full size with your current settings, even if you were looking at the small fast preview.

One colour list. The colours of the dots, outline and magnifier come from the colour list at the top of base.css, so changing a colour there changes it everywhere.

Keyboard. Click the photo, press 1 to 4 to pick a dot, then use the arrow keys to move it. Hold Shift to move 10 times further.

Under the hood

All the state lives in one settings object in main.js, and the drawing code never reads the page. invalidate({warp, preview}) gathers every change into a single requestAnimationFrame, so many changes in one frame cost one redraw.

The page moves through four phases: start, demo, ready and moved. Grabbing a dot during the demo cancels the animation.

Download always re-renders the normal backward scan at full size with the current look settings, then calls invalidate(), because the shared buffers were overwritten.

ProofLayers draws the grids, the matrix and the pixel tracer: init({sourceCanvas, flatCanvas, sourceOverlay, flatOverlay, cropCanvas, matrixEl, tracerInfoEl, mode}), update({h, flatWidth, flatHeight}) and setMode('warp' | 'crop'). It uses the engine's position_of_pixel(h, q, v), and marks the matrix as perspective or affine.

The colours of the dots, outline and magnifier are read from the CSS variables at startup, so :root in base.css is the single palette.

Keyboard: focus the photo, press 1 to 4 to pick a dot, and use the arrow keys to nudge it (hold Shift for 10 times further).
