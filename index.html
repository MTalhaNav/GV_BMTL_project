The story

A bit of detective work, in six steps.

  1. 1

    We start with a blank page

    Nothing yet, just an empty grid of pixels . We are turning a tilted page back into a flat one.

  2. 2

    Each pixel asks a question

    For every pixel on the flat page: where should I look in the photo to find my colour? But we have no directions yet. We need 8 numbers.

  3. 3

    The clues come from you

    Drag 4 dots onto the page corners. Each corner gives 2 clues, one for left and right and one for up and down. 4 corners give 8 clues, exactly enough to find 8 unknown numbers.

  4. 4

    Solving the clues into a map

    We solve them with Gaussian elimination , the elimination method from school, just bigger. The 8 numbers it finds are our map.

  5. 5

    Painting pixel by pixel

    The engine goes to each pixel of the flat page, uses the map to find its spot in the photo, notes the colour there and paints it in. Pixel by pixel, until the page is complete.

  6. 6

    Why go backwards?

    If we pushed photo pixels forward onto the page instead, some spots would never get a colour and the page would have holes. Asking backwards fills every pixel.

The extras

Three more things the scanner can do, and what each one shows.

  1. 1

    Greyscale: only the colour changes

    Every pixel stays exactly where it is. Its colour is pulled towards its own brightness, counting green most because our eyes are most sensitive to it, so the page turns into shades of grey.

  2. 2

    Plain crop: why cutting is not enough

    Cutting out the upright box around the dots looks like a quick fix, but the page inside is still tilted and the desk shows in the corners. That is why a scanner has to stretch the page, not just crop it.

  3. 3

    Forward mapping: going the wrong way

    Instead of each scan pixel asking where to look, every photo pixel is pushed to where it lands. Some spots are hit twice and some never, so the page is left full of holes.

How it works

There are Four parts to this Trick

1st

Four dots, eight equations

Each dot has an x and a y, and each of those is one equation. The matrix has eight unknown numbers, so four dots are exactly enough. Gaussian elimination solves them.

Your dots nowwaiting for the photo

2nd

One matrix sends any scan pixel to the photo

x = (h0·u + h1·v + h2) / (h6·u + h7·v + 1)
y = (h3·u + h4·v + h5) / (h6·u + h7·v + 1)

(u, v) is a spot on the flat page and (x, y) is where it sits in the photo. The shared bottom line is the perspective. Drag the dots into a parallelogram and it drops to zero.

Perspective termswaiting for the photo

3rd

Ask backwards, not forwards

For each scan pixel the matrix says where to look in the photo, and the colour is blended from the four nearest photo pixels. Pushing photo pixels forward instead leaves gaps.

4th

Then one rule per pixel

Greyscale pulls each colour towards its brightness. The black and white cut-off turns every pixel black or white around one number. Neither changes the shape.

Going deeper

The reasoning behind the choices.

Why eight numbers and not nine

A matrix like this is only defined up to scale. Multiply all nine numbers by the same amount and the mapping is identical, because the factor cancels in the division. So the last number is fixed at 1 and the other eight are solved for.

Why sample at pixel centres and blend four pixels

Photo pixel i has its centre at i + 0.5, so the lookup shifts by half a pixel before blending. Bilinear blending mixes the four surrounding pixels by how close the lookup point is to each one. It costs four reads per colour channel and avoids the jagged edges of copying the nearest pixel. While you drag, a smaller preview keeps it fast, sized to the speed of your device.

What goes wrong when the dots are wrong

The bottom line of the equations can reach zero, and where it does the position shoots off to infinity. A convex quad keeps it positive across the whole page. That is why the outline turns red and the scan freezes when the shape folds in or crosses itself.

What we left out: finding the corners automatically

Real scanner apps look for the page edges themselves, usually by detecting edges and picking the largest four-sided shape. We left it out on purpose. It is a separate problem from the stretching, and a wrong guess would hide the maths this page is about.

Matrix A grid of numbers. Here it holds the 8 numbers (plus a fixed 1) that map the flat page to the photo.
Perspective Things further from the camera look smaller. A tilted page looks narrower at the far end because of it.
Greyscale Turning every colour into a shade of grey that matches how bright it is.
Black and white (threshold) Every pixel brighter than a cut-off number becomes white, and every darker one becomes black.
Plain crop Cutting a rectangle out of the photo. The page is still tilted inside it.
Forward mapping Pushing each photo pixel onto the flat page. Some spots get no pixel, so the page has holes.
Enhance Makes the paper white and the ink vivid, like a scanner app, by evening out the light across the page.
Pixel One tiny square of colour. A photo is a grid of millions of them.
Gaussian elimination A way to solve many equations at once: remove one unknown at a time, then work backwards to find each one.
